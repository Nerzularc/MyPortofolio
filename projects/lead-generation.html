Brief (simulated client)

Client: "OpsBridge" (fictional), a remote operations-support agency offering research, admin and CRM upkeep to growing companies.
Goal: 100 researched target accounts across four markets, prioritised so the founder can start outreach with the best-fit 20.

Synthetic data notice. Every company in this database is invented. Names end in "(fictional)" and websites use the reserved .example domain, so they can never point to a real business. No real person's name, email or phone number appears anywhere. "Verification" values are simulated to demonstrate the workflow. They are not real checks.

Ideal customer profile (ICP)

IndustryB2B SaaS, digital and marketing agencies, EdTech, management and operations consulting
Size51–200 employees is the sweet spot: big enough to have process pain, too small for a full ops team. 11–50 and 201–500 are secondary.
GeographyUS, UK, Australia and Singapore. These are English-speaking, used to remote contractors, with workable time-zone overlap from Indonesia.
TriggersHiring for operations or admin roles; recent growth (new office, funding, headcount growth); already uses a CRM such as HubSpot.
ExclusionsCompanies with fewer than 10 staff, sole traders (consent rules differ, see outreach), direct competitors, and any company with an explicit "no solicitation" notice.

Buyer persona

"Operations-stretched Olivia", Head of Operations at a 120-person SaaS company

  • Goals: keep the CRM clean, ship weekly reporting on time and free senior staff from admin.
  • Pains: a headcount freeze, a backlog of research and data tasks, and context-switching across time zones.
  • Buying triggers: an ops role open for over 30 days, or a new market launch that needs research.
  • Objections: "Offshore quality is inconsistent" and "Onboarding takes too long".
  • What convinces her: a small paid pilot, written SOPs and a sample deliverable before signing.
  • Channels: LinkedIn, email and operator communities. She prefers short, specific messages.

The persona is a hypothesis built from typical job descriptions for this title. It should be validated by interviewing 5–10 real buyers.

Prospect research methodology

  1. Build the account universe from LinkedIn company search filters (industry, headcount, location), curated directories and Google search operators.
  2. Confirm firmographics on the company website (About, Careers, Contact pages) and record the source URL and access date.
  3. Capture signals: open ops roles on the careers page or job boards, CRM evidence such as HubSpot forms, and growth news.
  4. Identify the decision-maker title using the size and title map below. Names are added only from public professional profiles, and only for a real client.
  5. Score and tier with the model below, then send only qualified leads to verification.
  6. QA: spot-check 10% of rows against their source, de-duplicate on the website domain and normalise country and size values.

Email verification workflow

  1. Only verify qualified leads. Tier A and B leads only, to save credits.
  2. Find the business address from public company sources, or use a pattern (for example first.last@domain) marked as "pattern, unconfirmed".
  3. Run the address through a verification tool's free tier, which checks syntax, domain, MX record and mailbox where possible.
  4. Record the result: Valid (send), Catch-all (send in small batches and monitor bounces), Invalid (remove) or Unknown (re-check or find another channel).
  5. Keep the bounce rate low. Pause the campaign and re-verify if bounces rise above about 3%.
  6. Log the date of verification. Addresses decay as people change jobs, so re-verify after 90 days.

Outreach strategy

Sequence (Tier A)

  1. Day 1, email: a personalised first line referencing a real signal, one sentence of value and a soft call to action ("Worth a 15-min chat?").
  2. Day 3, LinkedIn: a connection request with no pitch.
  3. Day 6, email: a short follow-up with a relevant sample, such as a one-page research brief.
  4. Day 12, email: a breakup message that leaves the door open.

Compliance by market

These are summaries to brief a client. They are not legal advice, and a real campaign should be confirmed with the client's counsel.

  • United Kingdom (PECR): under PECR, sole traders and simple partnerships are treated as individuals and need consent, while corporate subscribers such as limited companies and LLPs can be emailed without prior consent. Every message still needs a working opt-out.
  • Australia (Spam Act 2003): commercial email needs express or inferred consent. Consent can be inferred when an address is conspicuously published without a "no marketing" statement, but only if the offer relates directly to the recipient's role or line of business. Unsubscribe and sender identification are required.
  • Singapore (Spam Control Act 2007 and PDPA): unsolicited commercial email sent in bulk must start its subject line with the label <ADV> and must offer an unsubscribe option.
  • United States (CAN-SPAM): include an accurate sender, a non-deceptive subject, a physical postal address and a working opt-out, and honour opt-outs promptly.

Sources: ICO guidance and UK legal commentary; legislation.gov.au and ALRC on the Spam Act; IMDA and the Singapore Spam Control Act. Full URLs are in the project document.
